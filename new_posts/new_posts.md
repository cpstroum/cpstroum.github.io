## Bridge Replacement.jpg

## Time Dilation.jpg
url: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cpstroum_i-was-on-pto-last-week-and-on-our-drive-share-7501134942517514240-6muA/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAELeooBB27kzMhffc041Rrtja8OZdXn3FE

I was on PTO last week, and on our drive to the Jersey Shore, I thought about complex program management.

(Yeah, I need better road trip entertainment.)

Think about it: as a driver, you're locked in. You've got traffic on the Garden State Parkway, construction, a missed exit or turn, the bathroom and fuel stop at Wawa, and the search for new radio stations in a rental car.

Our kids, on the other hand, experienced hours as passengers. It was so boring. When would we be there? My childhood radio station and some library books weren't enough of a distraction.

Organizations experience projects the same way.

A senior leader fumes, “We started this in March, and it’s September.” Six months. Why isn’t it done?

The team knows that March started discovery, April was spent waiting for contracts and access, May hit a data problem, June was architectural review, July was a rebuild due to bad design, and August included fourteen glorious days of real development.

The farther you are from the work, the more likely you are to mistake elapsed time for working time.

This is one of the reasons I often talk about not glamorizing the corporate ladder. Leaders at higher altitudes only see milestones and dates because they won't read real updates. The people lower on the ladder manage work as dependencies, decisions, interruptions, discoveries, and reviews.

A great leader will ask, “How can we make this faster next time?” instead of “Why has this taken six months?” A great performer will know to lead with those opportunities instead of defending the length of the program.

Let's say 80% of the time was spent waiting on approvals and dependencies. It's time to consider whether those policies are no longer a fit for the organization's scale and pace. Your members or customers don’t care that your security review took three weeks due to a redesign; they just care that it happened.

If you want to make the next trip faster, though, you need to know whether the problem was the driver, the traffic, or the route.

The next time something in your organization feels too slow, ask, “Where did the time go?” It's a lot better than “Are we there yet?”

## Pathfinder.jpg
url: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cpstroum_years-ago-i-saw-a-far-side-cartoon-that-share-7505646761433280512-xled/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAELeooBB27kzMhffc041Rrtja8OZdXn3FE

Years ago, I saw a Far Side cartoon that I reference a lot in my work.

A group is making its way through the jungle. The person in front has walked directly into a giant spiderweb; someone safely behind him quips that this is exactly why he never goes first.

This cartoon encapsulates the challenge of working with emerging technology.

Going first means getting the /web in your face/:
- You discover the tech that's straightforward but the people and processes unable to adapt to integrate it. 
- You'll build a model that performs ell until encounters a new enrollee with zero data to influence a risk score. 
- You'll invest in functionality that works but the governance outweighs the benefits. 
- You'll fall into the Soft Savings Trap, where a tool is an very expensive way to save twelve minutes.
- You'll even spend weeks hacking through a new problem only to discover that there is nothing useful on the other side.

Organizations need people whose job includes finding those dead ends.

That can be uncomfortable because we tend to evaluate emerging technology work using the language of delivery: What shipped? What scaled? What's the ROI? 

Failure is part of the portfolio, too; someone has to figure out what technology is mature enough to matter, where the economics work, what tiny implementation detail is easy to overlook, and which impressive demo collapses on contact with organizational reality.

These Pathfinders help to find a safe way for thousands of people to move.

The people behind you shouldn't have to fight through the same brush. They'll get the trail you cleared, the mistakes you documented, and hopefully a lot fewer bugs.

There is a place for this kind of work in most organizations, particularly right now in AI. Some paths and ibvestments will be dead ends; others will become paths and roads.

## Emotional Variable.jpg
url: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cpstroum_its-time-to-add-vibes-management-to-your-share-7508562543989420032-2yw-/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAELeooBB27kzMhffc041Rrtja8OZdXn3FE

It's time to add "vibes management" to your resume. 

Let's talk about emotional intelligence (EQ), the professional placement of "vibes". It's professional HVAC: the room does not need the same temperature all day, but neither does your team.

One of the most underrated leadership abilities is knowing the emotional temperature a situation needs and being able to change it:
- Not every meeting needs enthusiasm. Sometimes a team needs someone who makes a difficult problem feel manageable. 
- Not every problem needs urgency. 
- A stalled project may actually need some impatience.
- A contentious meeting may need curiosity or a well timed joke.
- Sometimes they need a leader willing to say, /yeah, this went badly/, without immediately converting the failure into an inspirational learning opportunity.

The nugget is that applying EQ means the ability to *set the room to the appropriate temperature* and not just read the room. 

I was reminded of this recently when I raised a concern with a leader. I came into the conversation *visibly* frustrated; he responded with enough optimism that I walked out seeing the problem differently.

Nothing really changed about the underlying situation in that call. The /vibes/ had, and it mattered more than I realized.

As AI gets better at doing more of the technical work, I think these small acts of human calibration become more conspicuous, not less.

You do not need sophisticated technology to do this. You need to notice the room, understand what the people in it need, and choose deliberately what you contribute to it.

## Trash Collection.jpg
url: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cpstroum_i-taught-my-daughters-about-tikkun-olam-share-7512255777575813121-A6Vx/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAELeooBB27kzMhffc041Rrtja8OZdXn3FE

I taught my daughters about /Tikkun Olam/, or repairing the world.

When we leave a park or playground, we try to leave it a little cleaner than we found it. A wrapper goes in the trash. An abandoned bottle gets recycled. Sometimes we talk about why people leave things behind in the first place.

I've been mistakenly applying this same philosophy to work: good intention but wrong implementation.

This week, I told my boss I would meet him after I kicked off a long-running job. The job is someone else's responsibility, but my incomplete handoff meant it hadn't been run. I had supplied instructions and followed up asynchronously but I wasn't getting a response.

I decided to run it myself and babysat it for hours, altering the code when it hit edge cases and I had to restart.

He followed up with a question I've never had to account for:

/Why are you becoming the garbage collector?/

It reminded me of a leadership lesson we use at SCAN: a leader's job isn't to carry every rock in the load; it's to break the load down and make sure the rocks have owners.

Repairing the world doesn't always mean picking up the thing someone else dropped; sometimes it means repairing the system that keeps dropping it.

By running the job myself, I was solving the immediate problem but weakening my impact as a  leader.

The better work was to establish:

• Who owns it
• When it needs to happen
• What a complete handoff looks like
• What happens when the handoff stalls
• When accountability moves from a polite async nudge to an actual conversation

Picking up trash with my daughters takes thirty seconds, but at work, picking up abandoned tasks can stall your development and prevent accountability of others.

## Plasticityv2.jpg
url: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cpstroum_in-my-senior-year-of-college-i-accidentally-ugcPost-7522351089451532288-y5E-/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAAELeooBB27kzMhffc041Rrtja8OZdXn3FE

In my senior year of college, I accidentally trapped myself in an ankle-foot orthotic (AFO) in my dorm room.

How did I get there?

The prior summer, I'd participated in a student exchange between Washington University in St. Louis and The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. We worked with students and the Jockey Rehabilitation Center on orthotics and rehabilitation, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).

Our work took us from Hong Kong into Guangzhou, where we met senior and pediatric patients and experienced the practical challenges of delivering this care.

To make an AFO, we made a negative cast of the patient's anatomy onsite. We went back to Hong Kong to fabricate the positive mold and formed the thermoplastic orthotic to that. The team would later return to Guangzhou for final fitting and adjustments. This travel and fabrication wasn't easy: during one fitting, we blew the circuit of the mainland facility we worked in.

We took these round-trips to heart: how could we anticipate adjustments earlier on? Could we incorporate channels for the TENS wires into the cast? Is it possible to remove the negative/positive elements and jump right to fabrication?

Back in St. Louis, we started our senior design projects. We rode the train to a local orthotics & prosthetics shop, where they let us practice with their equipment. We ultimately brought it to our dorm room to simulate a fabrication experience outside of the workshop. We just happened to overlook /removing the orthotic/.

The serrated bread knife didn't work and we had to buy shears from the hardware store to free me. A cast cutter isn't the kind of thing most stores stock.

The experiences of that trip and my senior design project showed me that the challenge of healthcare innovation isn't having a great idea; it's working within, or changing, the constraints of where you're delivering the care.

We battled the equipment, people's skills, the material cost, and the number of visits necessary to complete the job.

With this work, we learned the importance of designing for the delivery environment.
